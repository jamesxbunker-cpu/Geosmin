COM Ports: What They Are and How They Work

A plain-English guide to serial communication, from voltage levels to baud rates, and why they still matter.

September 20, 2026  |  Embedded, Serial, Hardware

Introduction

If you have ever plugged a microcontroller into your computer and watched a stream of text appear in a terminal window, you have used a COM port. They are one of the oldest and simplest ways for two devices to talk to each other, and despite decades of newer alternatives, they are still everywhere in embedded work.

In this post, I will explain what a COM port actually is, how serial data moves across the wire, what all those settings like baud rate and parity mean, and how I handled them in my ComPort project. No prior serial experience required.

What Is a COM Port?

A COM port is a serial communication endpoint on a Windows machine. The name comes from "communications port," and you will see them labeled COM1, COM2, COM3, and so on. On other operating systems, the same concept might be called a serial port, a TTY, or a /dev/tty device.

Serial means the data travels one bit at a time down a single wire (plus a ground reference and sometimes additional control lines). This is in contrast to parallel communication, where multiple bits travel simultaneously on separate wires. Serial won out for most modern use because it needs fewer wires, works over longer distances, and is far easier to route on a circuit board.

The physical hardware behind a COM port is usually a UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter). A UART is a small block of logic that takes bytes from your software, converts them into a timed sequence of voltage pulses, and does the reverse on the receiving end.

Key idea: A COM port is a software abstraction. Underneath, it might be a real UART chip on a motherboard, a USB-to-serial converter cable, or a virtual port created by a Bluetooth adapter. Your code talks to the COM port and does not need to care which one it is.

How Data Moves Across the Wire

Serial communication is asynchronous, which means there is no separate clock wire keeping the two sides in sync. Instead, both sides agree on a timing in advance, and the sender signals the start of each byte with a specific pattern.

Here is what a single byte looks like on the wire:

Idle (high) | START | D0 | D1 | D2 | D3 | D4 | D5 | D6 | D7 | STOP | Idle (high) | 0 | b0 | b1 | b2 | b3 | b4 | b5 | b6 | b7 | 1 |

Walking through each part:

  • Idle (high): When nothing is being sent, the line sits at a high voltage. This is the resting state.
  • Start bit: The sender pulls the line low for one bit period. This is the signal to the receiver that a byte is coming. The receiver watches for this falling edge.
  • Data bits: Typically 7 or 8 bits, sent least-significant bit first. These are the actual payload.
  • Parity bit (optional): A simple error check. It can be even, odd, or none. More on this below.
  • Stop bit: The sender returns the line to high for one or two bit periods. This gives the receiver a moment to prepare for the next start bit.

The receiver knows the timing because both sides agree on the baud rate beforehand. It waits half a bit period after detecting the start bit, samples the middle of each data bit, and then checks the stop bit. Sampling in the middle is important because that is where the signal is most stable, far from the edges where it transitions.

Why "asynchronous" matters: Since there is no shared clock, even a small timing mismatch between the two sides will accumulate. If the sender is running slightly fast and the receiver slightly slow, by the time the receiver gets to the last data bit, it might be sampling at the wrong moment. This is why baud rates must match closely, usually within about 2 to 3 percent.

Baud Rate: The Speed Setting

Baud rate is the number of signal changes per second on the line. For simple serial links, it also equals the number of bits per second, because each bit is one signal change.

Common baud rates you will see:

  • 9600: slow but extremely tolerant of noise and timing errors. Still common for GPS modules and older equipment.
  • 115200: the workhorse for microcontrollers. Fast enough for debug logs and sensor data, slow enough to be reliable.
  • 921600: fast. Used when you need to stream a lot of data, like high-rate IMU samples or firmware uploads.

What baud rate actually means: At 115200 baud, each bit lasts about 8.68 microseconds. A full byte with a start bit, 8 data bits, and a stop bit takes 10 bit periods, so about 86.8 microseconds. That works out to roughly 11,520 bytes per second, which is why 115200 is often quoted as "about 11.5 KB/s" in practice.

The number one serial bug: Mismatched baud rates. If your terminal is set to 9600 and your device is sending at 115200, you will see garbage characters, not a clean error message. The receiver will sample at the wrong times and produce nonsense. Always check the baud rate first when serial output looks wrong.

Framing: Data Bits, Parity, and Stop Bits

The combination of data bits, parity, and stop bits is called the framing. Both sides must agree on it, just like the baud rate.

The most common configuration is 8N1: 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit. You will see this written as 8N1 in datasheets and terminal settings. It is the default for almost everything.

Data bits: 8 is standard because it matches a byte. 7 is sometimes used for pure ASCII communication, where the high bit is never needed. 5 and 6 show up in very old equipment.

Parity: A single extra bit added after the data bits to catch simple errors.

  • None (N): No parity bit. The most common choice.
  • Even (E): The parity bit is set so the total number of 1s in the data plus parity is even.
  • Odd (O): Same idea, but the total must be odd.
  • Mark (M) / Space (S): The parity bit is always 1 or always 0. Rarely used.

Parity catches single-bit errors but cannot correct them and cannot detect multi-bit errors. In practice, most modern links use no parity and rely on higher-level protocols (like CRC checks) to catch errors.

Stop bits: 1, 1.5, or 2. One stop bit is standard. Two stop bits give the receiver a bit more time between bytes, which can help on slow or noisy links.

Flow Control: Preventing Overflow

If the sender transmits faster than the receiver can process, data gets lost. Flow control is the mechanism that tells the sender to pause.

There are two common types:

  • Hardware flow control (RTS/CTS): Two extra wires, Request to Send and Clear to Send. When the receiver is ready, it asserts CTS. When it needs the sender to pause, it drops CTS. This is fast and reliable because it happens in hardware.
  • Software flow control (XON/XOFF): Special control characters sent inside the data stream. XOFF (usually Ctrl+S) means pause, and XON (Ctrl+Q) means resume. This works with only three wires but is fragile because the control characters can be confused with real data.

In my ComPort project, I defaulted to no flow control because most microcontroller links do not need it at moderate baud rates. But for high-speed links or devices that cannot keep up, hardware flow control is worth enabling.

When flow control matters: If you are losing bytes intermittently at high baud rates, flow control is one of the first things to check. Without it, the sender has no way to know the receiver's buffer is full.

Using COM Ports in Windows

On Windows, a COM port is treated like a file. You open it with CreateFile, passing a name like COM3 or \\.\COM10. Yes, you read that correctly: the same function used to open files is used to open serial ports.

HANDLE hCom = CreateFile( "\\\\.\\COM3", GENERIC_READ | GENERIC_WRITE, 0, NULL, OPEN_EXISTING, FILE_ATTRIBUTE_NORMAL, NULL );

Why the \\.\ prefix? Windows has a special device namespace. Names like \\.\COM3 tell the system "this is a device, not a file in the current directory." For ports numbered 10 and above, the prefix is required because plain COM10 would be interpreted differently. It is a common source of confusion.

Once the port is open, you configure it with a DCB structure (Device Control Block). This is where you set the baud rate, data bits, parity, stop bits, and flow control. Then you call SetCommState to apply the settings.

DCB dcb = {0}; dcb.DCBlength = sizeof(dcb); GetCommState(hCom, &dcb); dcb.BaudRate = CBR_115200; dcb.ByteSize = 8; dcb.Parity = NOPARITY; dcb.StopBits = ONESTOPBIT; dcb.fBinary = TRUE; dcb.fParity = FALSE; SetCommState(hCom, &dcb);

After that, reading and writing is done with ReadFile and WriteFile, exactly like a file. You can also set timeouts with SetCommTimeouts so reads do not block forever if no data arrives.

The CBR_115200 constant: The Windows headers define named constants for common baud rates. You can also pass the raw number, like 115200, and it will usually work. The named constants exist mainly for historical reasons and for clarity.

Pitfalls I Ran Into

Serial programming has a few classic traps. Here are the ones that caught me:

  • The \\.\ prefix. My first attempt to open COM10 failed with a file not found error. I spent twenty minutes checking cables before realizing I needed \\.\COM10. Ports 1 through 9 work without the prefix, which makes the problem appear only when you go past 9.
  • Blocking reads that never return. By default, ReadFile on a COM port blocks until at least one byte arrives. If the device is silent, your thread hangs forever. The fix is to set proper timeouts with SetCommTimeouts, or to use overlapped I/O with a separate wait.
  • Assuming a read returns a full buffer. ReadFile can return fewer bytes than you asked for. It fills whatever is available and tells you how many bytes it got. If you assume it always returns the requested amount, you will drop data. The fix is to always check the returned byte count and accumulate data until you have a complete message.
  • Baud rate mismatch after reconnecting. Some USB-to-serial adapters reset their settings when the cable is unplugged and replugged. My code assumed the port kept its configuration, which led to garbage output after a reconnect. The fix was to reapply the DCB settings every time the port is opened.
  • Not flushing on close. If you close a COM port while data is still in the transmit buffer, that data can be lost. Calling FlushFileBuffers before closing gives the driver a chance to drain the buffer. It is a small thing, but it matters when you are logging data.

Common Serial Settings at a Glance

Setting Common Value Notes
Baud rate 115200 Most microcontrollers default to this
Data bits 8 Matches a byte exactly
Parity None Rely on higher-level checks instead
Stop bits 1 Two is rare, used for slow links
Flow control None Enable RTS/CTS if you drop bytes

When in doubt, start with 115200 8N1 and no flow control. That combination works with the vast majority of hobby and embedded devices.

Summary

COM ports are a simple, reliable way to move data between devices. The key points:

  • A COM port is a serial endpoint, usually backed by a UART that converts bytes to timed voltage pulses.
  • Serial is asynchronous: no shared clock, so both sides must agree on the baud rate in advance.
  • Each byte is framed with a start bit, data bits, an optional parity bit, and one or more stop bits.
  • Framing is written as a shorthand like 8N1: 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit.
  • Flow control (RTS/CTS or XON/XOFF) prevents the sender from overrunning the receiver.
  • On Windows, you open a COM port with CreateFile using the \\.\ prefix, configure it with a DCB, and read and write with ReadFile and WriteFile.

Serial is old, but it is not going away. USB-to-serial adapters, debug consoles, GPS modules, and countless sensors all speak it. Understanding the basics saves you hours of confusion when something does not work.
