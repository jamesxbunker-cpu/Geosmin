Madgwick, Mahony, and Kalman Filters

Three ways to estimate 3D orientation from noisy sensors, and when to use each one.

September 14, 2026  |  Embedded, Sensors, Algorithms

Introduction

If you've ever worked with an IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit), you've faced the same problem: no single sensor gives you a reliable orientation estimate on its own. Gyroscopes drift. Accelerometers are noisy. Magnetometers get distorted by nearby metal. The solution is to combine them: that's exactly what the Madgwick, Mahony, and Kalman filters do.

In this post, I'll explain the core problem these filters solve, walk through each one's approach, and compare them so you can pick the right tool for your project. I will be using one of these for my future IMU-based projects, and I hope this post helps you choose the right one for yours.

The Core Problem: Gyroscope Drift

A gyroscope measures angular velocity. If you integrate that over time, you get orientation. But gyroscopes have a constant bias error, and integration accumulates that error. After a minute, your estimate might be 10 degrees off. This is called drift.

What "integrating" actually means here: If you know you're rotating at 90 degrees per second, then after one second you've rotated 90 degrees. After two seconds, 180 degrees. That's integration: adding up angular velocity over time to get total angle. The problem is that if the gyro says "90 degrees per second" when it's really "89.5 degrees per second," that tiny 0.5 degree error gets added every single second. After a minute, you think you've rotated 5400 degrees when you've actually rotated 5370. That 30-degree gap is drift.

An accelerometer measures gravity direction. It doesn't drift. But it's noisy and useless when the device is accelerating (like on a drone with vibrating propellers).

A magnetometer measures Earth's magnetic field and provides absolute yaw, but it's distorted by nearby metal or electronics.

Every filter here combines these sensors. The question is how.

Madgwick Filter: Gradient Descent

Madgwick uses a mathematical technique called gradient descent to correct the gyroscope's drift. It computes the error between "where gravity should be" (based on the current orientation estimate) and "where gravity actually is" (from the accelerometer), then takes a small step in the direction that reduces that error.

What gradient descent means in plain English: Imagine you're standing on a foggy hill and want to reach the bottom. You can't see the whole landscape, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, check the slope again, and repeat. Eventually you reach the bottom. Gradient descent does the same thing with numbers: it looks at the current error, figures out which direction would reduce it, and takes a small step that way. In Madgwick's case, the "hill" is the mismatch between predicted gravity and measured gravity, and the "bottom" is zero error.

The key equation looks like this:

q̇ = ½ · q ⊗ (0, ω) − β · ∇f/‖∇f‖

Breaking down that equation piece by piece:

  • q̇ (read "q-dot") — the rate of change of orientation. It's what we want to compute.
  • ½ · q ⊗ (0, ω) — the gyroscope term. q is the current orientation (stored as a quaternion, which is just a compact way to represent 3D rotation with four numbers instead of nine). The ⊗ symbol means "quaternion multiplication." (0, ω) packs the three angular velocity readings into a quaternion-like structure. This whole term says: "if only the gyroscope existed, here's how orientation would change."
  • − β · ∇f/‖∇f‖ — the correction term. ∇f (read "grad f") is the gradient: a vector pointing in the direction of steepest error increase. Dividing by ‖∇f‖ (its magnitude) normalizes it to a unit vector, so only the direction matters, not the size. The minus sign means we go the opposite way, toward lower error. β is a tuning knob: how big a step do we take each time?
The big advantage: Madgwick is extremely lightweight. The correction step is just "a handful of multiplies" because the gradient has an analytic form, no matrix inversions, no heavy math. It runs at kilohertz speeds on microcontrollers. The original open-source implementation is freely available, and STM32 ports exist.

The downside: It converges unpredictably in some cases, and its yaw estimate degrades badly in magnetically disturbed environments because it relies on the magnetometer for heading. This can be problematic depending on your application.

Mahony Filter: PI Controller

Mahony takes a different approach. Instead of gradient descent, it uses a proportional-integral (PI) controller to drive the estimated gravity direction toward the measured direction.

What a PI controller is: Think of cruise control in a car. You set a target speed (say 100 km/h). The car measures its actual speed and compares it to the target. The difference is the "error." A PI controller reacts to that error in two ways:

  • P (proportional): "The faster I'm going, the more I ease off the gas." Big error means big correction. Small error means small correction. This handles the immediate response.
  • I (integral): "If I've been consistently 5 km/h too slow for a while, I should gradually press harder on the gas." This term accumulates past errors and fixes long-term bias. It's what eliminates steady-state error.

Here's the intuition: the cross product between estimated gravity and measured gravity gives you a rotation axis and magnitude. The PI controller uses that error to adjust the gyroscope readings before integration.

What a cross product is: If you have two arrows in 3D space, the cross product gives you a third arrow that's perpendicular to both. Its length depends on how far apart the two original arrows are. In Mahony's case, one arrow is "which way I think gravity points" and the other is "which way the accelerometer says gravity points." If they're perfectly aligned, the cross product is zero (no correction needed). If they're misaligned, the cross product tells you which axis to rotate around to bring them back together, and how big that rotation should be.

The P term reacts to current error (fast correction), and the I term accumulates error over time to eliminate steady-state bias.

The advantage: Mahony is conceptually simpler than Madgwick, and the PI gains (Kp and Ki) have clear physical meanings: Kp controls responsiveness, Ki controls how aggressively it corrects long-term bias. Memory and computation are the lowest of the three options.

The downside: Like Madgwick, it also struggles with magnetic disturbances. One study found that both Madgwick and Mahony (along with a standard Kalman filter) produced "markedly different results" in magnetically disturbed locations compared to clean ones, indicating degraded performance.

Kalman Filter: Optimal Estimator

The Kalman filter is fundamentally different from the other two. It doesn't just blend sensor readings; it maintains a statistical model of the system, including:

  • A state vector (orientation, possibly velocity and position)
  • A covariance matrix representing uncertainty in that state
  • Process noise (how unpredictable the system is)
  • Measurement noise (how noisy each sensor is)

What a state vector is: It's just a list of numbers describing everything you care about right now. For orientation, it might be [roll, pitch, yaw, gyro_bias_x, gyro_bias_y, gyro_bias_z]. That's it: a compact snapshot of the system's current condition.

What a covariance matrix is: This is the part that trips most people up, so let's go slowly. A covariance matrix is a grid of numbers that answers the question: "How uncertain am I about each part of the state, and how do those uncertainties relate to each other?"

Imagine a 2D state with just roll and pitch. The covariance matrix would be a 2×2 grid:

[ σ²_roll cov(roll, pitch) ] [ cov(pitch, roll) σ²_pitch ]
  • σ²_roll — how uncertain you are about roll alone. Big number means "I'm not very sure."
  • σ²_pitch — same, for pitch.
  • cov(roll, pitch) — whether errors in roll and pitch tend to happen together. If this is positive, when roll is overestimated, pitch tends to be overestimated too. If zero, they're independent.

The filter keeps this matrix updated as it runs. When it makes a prediction, uncertainties grow (because you're extrapolating into the future). When it gets a measurement, uncertainties shrink (because new information helps).

At each timestep, the Kalman filter:

  1. Predicts the next state using the system model. This is like saying "based on how I was moving, here's where I expect to be now." Uncertainty grows because you're guessing.
  2. Compares that prediction to the actual measurement. The difference is called the "innovation," and it tells you how wrong your prediction was.
  3. Weights the correction based on how uncertain each source is. This is the "Kalman gain": if the prediction is uncertain but the measurement is clean, trust the measurement more. If the prediction is confident but the measurement is noisy, trust the prediction more.

If your orientation estimate is already very precise but the accelerometer reading is noisy, the filter barely adjusts. If the orientation estimate is uncertain and the accelerometer is clean, it adjusts a lot.

The advantage: The Kalman filter is mathematically optimal, it produces the minimum-variance estimate for linear systems with Gaussian noise. It can also estimate things the other filters don't, like velocity and position, by including them in the state vector.

The downside: It's computationally expensive. The standard Kalman filter requires matrix operations (multiplications, inversions) every update cycle.

Why matrix operations are expensive: A matrix is a grid of numbers, and multiplying two matrices isn't as simple as multiplying each pair of numbers. For every entry in the result, you multiply a whole row by a whole column and add up the products. A 6×6 matrix multiplication (typical for a 6-state Kalman filter) involves 216 individual multiplications, and that's before you get to the inversion step. Matrix inversion is even worse: it's like solving a system of equations where every equation depends on every other one. On a microcontroller running at 72 MHz with no floating-point unit, this can take milliseconds per update.

For nonlinear systems like orientation (which involves trigonometry and quaternion multiplication), you need the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), which adds Jacobian matrix computations on top of that.

What a Jacobian is: In plain terms, it's a matrix of derivatives. Derivatives tell you how much one thing changes when another thing changes. If you have a function that takes 6 inputs and produces 6 outputs, the Jacobian is a 6×6 grid where each entry answers: "if I nudge input #3 a tiny bit, how much does output #5 move?" The EKF needs this grid because its system model is nonlinear, so the relationship between inputs and outputs isn't a simple constant scaling. Computing the Jacobian means doing calculus analytically (or approximating it numerically) and then multiplying those matrices every single update. On a microcontroller, this can be a significant load.

Quick Comparison

Filter Method Computation Best For
Madgwick Gradient descent Low Fast embedded systems, open-source projects
Mahony PI controller Lowest Memory-constrained devices
Kalman Statistical estimator High Applications needing velocity/position too

The trade-off is clear: Madgwick and Mahony sacrifice optimality for speed, while Kalman gives you the best estimate at the cost of CPU cycles.

A Practical Note on Magnetic Disturbances

All three filters use the magnetometer for yaw estimation. In real environments magnetic fields get distorted: indoors, near motors, on drones. One recent study compared these filters and found they all degraded significantly in magnetically disturbed areas, while a newer algorithm (GMVDK) maintained consistent performance. If your application operates near electronics or metal, this is worth considering.

Summary

Choosing between Madgwick, Mahony, and Kalman comes down to your constraints:

  • Madgwick: best balance of accuracy and speed for most embedded projects. Open-source, well-documented, and runs comfortably on an STM32.
  • Mahony: the lightest option. Good when you're tight on memory or CPU, and the PI gains give you intuitive control over behavior.
  • Kalman: the most powerful, but the heaviest. Use it when you need velocity or position estimates alongside orientation, and you have the CPU headroom to afford it.

For a typical STM32 project doing basic orientation sensing, Madgwick or Mahony will serve you well. Reach for the Kalman filter when you need more than just orientation, or when you're willing to trade computation for optimality.
